## Juggling in China

Last May, I had the opportunity to visit China. I traveled by the Trans-Siberian Express. Of course, along with my "Handbook for Railway Enthusiasts" and my Chinese phrasebook, I packed plenty of beanbags and juggling balls.

We stayed overnight in Moscow. After juggling in the Kremlin for a few minutes, I had to queue for two hours to get tickets for the famous Moscow State Circus. That evening, Sergei Ignatov was unfortunately not on the program, but I saw an impressive juggler who juggled five rings, with a juggling monkey as his partner!

After a night in Moscow, we traveled by train for seven days. It's hardly possible to juggle on a train because there's too little space, but at every station, I jumped out onto the platform to practice a bit, much to the surprise of some Soviet citizens.

In Beijing (Peking), we started with an intensive tour, through temples, palaces, parks, and pagodas. During the exciting travel itinerary, I always found time to juggle. A European standing on a street corner already attracts twenty spectators. If he eats a biscuit, the number grows to fifty; if he starts juggling, up to two hundred people crowd around him! The Chinese people, from children to old men leaning on their bicycles, really enjoyed it. Everyone smiled at the jokes and applauded my five balls.

Many wanted to join in, and it was no problem to persuade the spectators to participate. I never once passed a hat (or a cap with a red star) around, as I found such a thing completely inappropriate for a European in a developing country.

**(Image: A man juggling beanbags in front of a building.)**
Photo: Jeremy Twyman

I saw some Chinese street performers, but no jugglers. I saw one who let a snake slide into his nose and bring it out through his mouth again! In the Beijing Youth Acrobatics Theater, there were jugglers. We saw foot juggling, balancing, unicycling, etc. The following afternoon, I went back to the theater and met many young artists. They found it hilarious when I started juggling with my beanbags. Although we couldn't communicate, we could throw and catch together. I understood their request for seven balls, but unfortunately, I had to decline!

Juggling is a wonderful way to overcome cultural barriers, and I thoroughly enjoyed my visit to China. I hope the Chinese people enjoyed it too!

Sam Scurfield, Bradford, England

P.S. I have just returned from Leningrad, where I met an excellent juggler, Alexander Kusmin. After seeing him in a cabaret, I befriended him, and we became good friends. He told me about his time as a student at the Moscow State Circus. He only knew one Western juggler, Anthony Gatto, but he was eager to learn more. He, in turn, told stories about Sergei Ignatov and others. We exchanged props and addresses. A great juggler, a good friend.

## An American in Europe

Five days before the European Meet, I didn't know I'd be juggling in Frankfurt the following week. Ideas come and plans change. Jon Wee, my partner in our group, "The 2 of Clubs," decided to go to school this year, and I didn't want that. After surprising my friends and family with my sudden decision, I spent two hectic days preparing, planning, and packing for my trip.

I flew to Luxembourg with Iceland Air (a big mistake). I had to change planes four times from Minneapolis, and my luggage was lost for 3 days, but luckily, I had most of my juggling stuff in my carry-on. It was like a dream. I felt like a science fiction character on a new adventure. I had a map, some saved money, no plans (except for the juggling meet), and a good imagination. The juggling meet turned out to be the best I've ever attended. The atmosphere was very relaxed and casual, yet full of energy and entrepreneurial spirit. I met many people from all over Europe and got many addresses.

I spent the first week in Wiesbaden with Uli Meister and Michael Bonnet. Of course, I was too heavily packed, so I left half my things there while I traveled around.

On the first two Saturdays, I discovered the possibility of financing myself through street shows and tried to improve my act. I had always worked in a group before, and these were my first solo performances. I had to overcome some language barriers. I can speak German reasonably well, so it went okay in Germany, but in France, Holland, Italy, and Norway, I tried to do silent shows, with a few translated keywords. Occasionally, I also used English.

I had saved some money from my juggling at the Colorado and Minnesota Renaissance Festivals last summer, but I decided not to use it if possible, as long as I could extend my trip through juggling. When I arrived in a new country, I exchanged 50 DM into the new currency and had to make ends meet by juggling. In Germany, Paris, and London, this was quite easy. As soon as I found the best spot to perform, a place for my luggage, and a place to stay overnight, I could earn enough money to live and still have enough time to make my trip a vacation.

For me, the best place to perform was in Germany's pedestrian zones or in a city where a festival was taking place. In Paris and London, there were many artists, and you had to work hard to get a good spot and a good time.

From December to January, I didn't do any street shows because I didn't need the money, but I saw and spoke with others who still earned enough despite the cold weather, especially around Christmas.

Everywhere, people were very friendly to me and cheered me up when I felt lonely on my solo journey. In every city where I sought out jugglers to train with, there were some who did everything they could to make it possible. I was also impressed by the juggling meets all over Europe. In Wiesbaden, Mannheim, Amsterdam, and Copenhagen, there were 10 to 20 people who met every week. In London and Berlin, there were at least two meets with over 25 people.

**(Image: A man juggling clubs in a circle of people.)**
Kaj during a circle game in Frankfurt Photo: Anthony Olins

A perfect example of this juggling brotherhood/sisterhood I experienced in Copenhagen when I arrived by train from Oslo at 7 AM. I called Erik and Susanne and said, "Hello, I'm a juggler. I was wondering if it might be possible for us to meet and juggle together; I'm here for a day." This led to a good breakfast, and I met over a dozen jugglers. They arranged for a gym for juggling (specially because I was there), and we juggled for 4 hours. Then I had a good dinner at Per and Bina's, also jugglers, and was taken to my night train to Hamburg at the station.

Thanks to everyone who contributed to making my trip so wonderful.
If anyone wants to visit me on their trip to America, come on over.

Kaj Fjelstad

## VENUE STREET: Morocco
by Danny Avrutick

Southern Morocco has a long tradition in all areas of street performance. Snake charmers, storytellers, musicians, comedians, acrobats, herbal medicine vendors, as well as religious preachers and teachers of sciences, including dentistry. All can be observed at work in the dusty squares at the souks. Some use improvised megaphones powered by car batteries and arrange props and strange objects in front of them on carpets to lend an aura of authority to their image.

They are always surrounded by a large crowd. The Moroccan audience is patient and attentive. It seems they will stand and watch any street performer for ages. The street entertainers work hard, long hours under the hot sun, often in large groups, often for a pittance. They don't just gather a circle around themselves, entertain, pass the hat, and send people away. Instead, the crowd settles in front of the performer, and from time to time, he goes around to squeeze donations out of people with all sorts of pleas, rhetoric, and invocations of "Allah." He makes funny, sarcastic remarks when a particularly large coin is given, and threats to quit if the proceeds are meager.

I spent my first seven weeks in Morocco observing and studying the dialect, while sometimes practicing juggling on the roofs of cheap hotels or on the beach. Sometimes I would have a session with the local musicians on my plastic flute or show juggling tricks to a few people in a private circle. After this time, despite some prior knowledge of various Arabic dialects, I still couldn't claim to truly understand the meaning of what was being said.

Many of the street performers seem to talk nonsense, building everything up endlessly without ever reaching a real performance. But the audience sits there forever, and I couldn't claim to have grasped the subtlety of this procedure. Especially the magicians don't seem to reach for their rusty Chinese Rings or their tattered containers at all. But all requests for reaction or audience participation are immediately followed. The performers often begin to pray, holding out their cupped hands or raising their right hand, and the audience fully participates. Once, I held up 5 fingers to signal my intention to juggle with 5 beanbags, and in no time, 5 young volunteers were at my side!

Well, having made my way as a street musician and juggler for 13 years, it was inevitable that I would also complete my Moroccan experiences in this direction.

After observing the other performers, I wrote down a speech in Arabic, which I memorized. I said: "I come from Washington D.C., I have been traveling through various countries for many years, trying to learn something about their cultures and ways of life, and also to share my art with them."

In addition, I translated (with the help of a local) some standard juggling jokes and memorized them too. Honestly, I never felt truly understood, but over time, the mysterious Arabic humor would surely have opened up to me as well!

Of course, I paid special attention to the collection speeches, pleading in the Arabic style: "See how hard I have worked, and the sun is hot, and see how tired I am; these arts require years of practice, and although you may think I am a rich American, I assure you, I am just a poor, simple man like you... And I know you are poor, but if each of you would give me a small coin, blablabla... and the blessing of Allah will be with you."

The few shows I performed on the plaza and the souk in Agadir and at the camel market in Goulimine went well. The people behaved well, and there was a lot of spontaneous applause. I naturally had to adapt to the Moroccan pace and spirit of timelessness, and it was pleasant not to feel the pressure I had when performing in Europe, where you have to play with a lot of fanfare and power, or they'll leave at the first dropped club. The collected 2 to 3 dollars in dirhams were quite a lot by local standards and were enough to pay for a cheap hotel bed and food for a day. As always, it's nice to have your pockets full of coins again when you're used to it.

**(Image: A group of performers in a circle, some with musical instruments.)**
Photo: Jugglers World

One more thing about street shows in Morocco: you can't just say, "Okay, that's it," and leave. People watch just as intently as you pack up your things, and you almost have to fight your way out of the circle to then slip away and hide. But all in all, it wasn't as bad as I had expected.

After a few shows on the "Place Jemma el Fna" in Marrakech, I was adopted and managed by a local acrobat troupe. I decided to go along with it to see what would happen. They hypnotically beat their drums, talked endlessly, got the audience in the mood, then I juggled, and after further speeches and appeals, they let me go around with a drum to collect, while the troupe leader stood next to me, talking to the audience: "Come on, help this poor boy," and encouraged me to keep going around to milk coins from these poor, ragged people.

I had fully expected the acrobats to keep all the money for themselves and at most invite me for a cup of tea, but to my great surprise, it turned out they wanted to give me everything, even though they themselves had worked hard to earn it. I then shared it 50/50.

Life is hard for the people here in Morocco, and they toil everywhere for a few dirhams. But as always, it's nice to see that good, hardworking street performers can make a living and often have a lot of fun doing it.

This report was given to us at the European Juggling Week in Frankfurt.

## BELGIUM '85 - Contagious!

Everything you ever wanted to know about the juggling meet in Louvain La Neuve and its organizers but were afraid to ask

Once upon a time, there was a student named Vincent Wauters, at a university near Brussels, in the Kingdom of Belgium. He was afflicted with a strange illness; he liked to throw things in the air and catch them again after they had described increasingly crazy trajectories.

Well, this illness was contagious, so he was soon surrounded by a group of infected followers. The university became concerned and provided the group with a hall one evening a week to study the illness and its spread.

Nothing could contain the virus, and eventually, the most severe cases were placed in quarantine in a large house, "Kot Jonglerie." Some time later, Vincent Wauters finished his studies, burned his diploma, and ran off to join a troupe of chronic patients, the "Cirque du Trottoir."

Meanwhile, Kot Jonglerie continued, under the direction of Eddy Krzeptowski, Philippe Van de Weghe, and Binet Valere, for whom there was long no hope of recovery, infecting the university and putting on amateur shows with the "Jolis Brius." All these cursed individuals had one goal: to infect as many people as possible. So, Vincent Wauters' sick brain hatched the devilish idea to found a circus school with Eddy and Philippe. And thus, the "Ecole sans Filet" (School without a Net) was born. Through the school, illnesses like juggling, unicycling, tightrope walking, acrobatics, clowning, mime, magic... began to spread ever faster.

When all these incurable individuals had finished their studies, they left behind an entire army of infected students to carry on the cause.

Eddy went to Cirque du Trottoir, and the Ecole sans Filet developed well. They missed no opportunity to attract public attention.

In 1983, shortly before the 6th European Juggling Meet, these crazy people hatched the idea of gathering hundreds of similarly obsessed individuals. But that year, the Wiesbaden sanatorium was preferred. That was no problem; it gave them another year to finalize their plans. So well, in fact, that they won the election in Frankfurt unanimously, although they had no opponents. Since then, the poor unfortunates have been preparing a meet that will go down in the history of medicine and epidemiology. As a small preview, here is a sneak peek:

When? September 5th, 6th, 7th, 8th, 1985

Where? Louvain La Neuve, Blocry Sports Center (a beautiful large hall)

What will happen?

*   Free juggling, as usual
*   Workshops: We will try to organize original and interesting workshops, not just juggling, but also related art forms: magic, acrobatics, unicycling... If you have other ideas or want to give a workshop, write to us.
*   Parade and competitions: On Saturday afternoon, we will all meet on the famous, magnificent Grand Place in Brussels. There, we will have a big parade with fun games, races, unicycle games, and street shows. Start preparing something for this grand happening now, which will brighten the day of every Brussels citizen.
*   Non-stop video show: We will show great juggling acts throughout the entire meet. You can contribute your own tapes.
*   Public show: It will be on Friday evening. 6 musicians will accompany the show; you can also bring your own music cassette.
*   Super Music-Hall Show: A truly great event on Saturday evening. You will have the chance to admire about 10 of the greatest jugglers, showcasing various aspects of the art. We expect top professionals. This show will, of course, be open to the public, to whom we hope to open their eyes to our art. To round off the unforgettable evening, we will all do a fantastic fire show. Don't forget your props.

## KASKADE workshop: PATTERNS

"Before you spend money, read these words! This book has no plot, no heroine, no surprising resolution; it is not for minors. It is not light reading. It will never appear in a condensed version in Reader's Digest. Patterns was written with a specific readership in mind: formation jugglers. It will show you how to pass clubs, or at least help you. It will introduce you to hundreds of group juggling patterns. If you are truly enthusiastic about passing and want to do and learn a lot about it, then this book was written for you."

With these words, Richard Dingman deters all potential buyers who might mistakenly think this is just another juggling instruction manual. This is not to say that you have to be a super-passer to get something out of "Patterns." On the contrary, it starts with the basics:

**(Image: 30(木 IkehirleyIzzieBorisand Cookie)**
2 people 1 club ("2 p(eople) 1c(lub)"). And with this combination, 2p 1c, the reader is guided through all the basic throws that form the building blocks for the complex patterns described later. Even if you can do all this with your right hand, the idea of being able to do some of these later patterns eventually is enough incentive to learn every throw with your left hand as well.

"You sometimes feel totally idiotic doing this, but the faster you become ambidextrous, the better, and sometimes it's quite good training for real life to feel idiotic." (p. 50)

Ambidexterity is indeed the key to incredible things you never thought possible. With as much empathy and patience as can be conveyed through the written word, Dingman encourages you to overcome the psychological barriers created by the complexity of the patterns and the unfamiliar movements (especially the left-handed ones). But Dingman proves to be a good teacher not only in his humorous writing style but also in the clarity with which he presents complicated things. For anyone who has stomped their clubs in frustration and anger because they couldn't explain to their partners what pattern they wanted to try, it's a given that a clear and universally understood language is needed for such things. For those with a passable knowledge of English who buy the book, this language will be Dingman's "Jugglish." Especially his counting system (which he didn't invent, but probably was the first to systematically write down) is a very valuable contribution to clarifying juggling communication. It's easy to learn and apply, and soon you'll wonder how you ever managed without it!

Where language reaches its limits, Judy Gailen's "Inchlings" come to the rescue, "to demonstrate all the patterns for our edification. They have a remarkable talent for juggling and obedience." These little figures are placed in all sorts of configurations and juggle an unlimited number of clubs. They are particularly helpful in clarifying patterns that involve many people and/or objects.

And it is precisely such patterns that are the greatest strengths of this book. (However, there are also plenty of suggestions for just two jugglers with a total of 3 clubs, for example). But the Inchlings won't teach you how to do trick throws. These, such as the "finger roll pass" or the "French tennis pass," are listed, but Dingman points out that they can only be learned through observation and experimentation.

Although many of the described patterns are excellent for a spectacular show, this aspect of performance possibilities is omitted and left to your own creativity. Rightly so, because following instructions for performance numbers leads to stereotypes. Instead, Dingman emphasizes the fun it can be to try out his suggestions, regardless of who is watching. In this spirit, he suggests some clearly non-Olympic games, e.g., this one for 2 people facing each other and playing with three clubs: "The rules are as follows: everything is allowed, but the throws must be good (at least good enough), and you must keep the rhythm. Slowing down or speeding up is therefore forbidden. If you drop or throw badly, you lose a point. If you lose 10 points, someone can bite off your toe."

Patterns is very heavy to transport (over 450 pages), but once you've finally lugged the book to the training hall, it's very easy and practical to use. Since it's spiral-bound, it stays open to the correct page while you practice.
You can order one of only 200 copies directly from the author by sending him $35 (including postage to Europe). Since the book is useless if you don't have a juggling partner, you might consider asking your friends to share both the cost and the fun.

Dr. P. Luftiko

## Book Review: **Juggling Art Through the Ages**

Excerpts from K.-H. Ziethen's complete work: "4000 Years of Juggling History"

From the outside, the book looks quite unassuming. DIN A4 size, beige, with a simple title and a drawing. Almost like a school notebook. I open the notebook, and lively images jump out at me from every page. So, first, I look at the pictures; that's how I like to start a book. Photos of jugglers, posters, and drawings. Jugglers on foot, on horseback, on a tightrope, with cannonballs, tables, spoons, and countless other things that people have thrown into the air over the centuries.

The pictures show all the "changes" in the art of juggling, from the type and number of objects juggled to the presentation. A book for browsing, looking, and marveling, a fascinating history of juggling in pictures, even for people who don't like to read.

I want to read the book too, so back to the beginning. The beginning is 1900 BC in Egypt. Really! Juggling has been around for at least that long. It's followed by countless pieces of information about where, how, and who has ever juggled on this earth. In the Roman Empire, they juggled with glass balls and breadcrumbs; the old Norwegian King Olaf Fryggeson juggled with lances, and in 1753, Anthony Maddox juggled six balls on a slack rope. And so it continues, over 36 pages. Is there any other famous juggler who hasn't been mentioned here? Probably not. But sometimes I would have preferred to read more short life stories about individual jugglers rather than just seeing many names listed, which remain just names without a story.

Such a 36-page book cannot and must not be complete, especially since it is a compilation of excerpts from "4000 Years of Juggling," Karl-Heinz Ziethen's mammoth work of 760 pages with 600 photos, previously only published in English. Unfortunately, I don't know this complete work, but I am sure that the unassuming school notebook provides an almost equally impressive picture of juggling history. And at a price that every juggler can afford. And in a language that every German-speaking juggler can understand without difficulty!

Gabi Hartmann

## News from LONDON
by Charlie Holland

1985 started well for jugglers in London, with two very entertaining and skilled shows as part of the 8th International London Mime Festival, by groups that incorporate a lot of juggling into their program. These groups were Ra Ra Zoo and Plexus Mime Theatre.

The Daily Telegraph wrote the review of Ra Ra Zoo (see below), which summarizes the core of the show well. The clean club passing routine includes, for example: Dave Spathaky (formerly one half of the Amazing Mendizies Juggling Duo) tries to join Sue and Stephen's passing, which only creates chaos; a 3-person line with 10 clubs, Sue in the middle throws double twists over her shoulder to Dave. Dave exchanges with Sue and turns 90° several times, maintaining the pattern. The show ends with 9 torches, played in the same pattern. All three shows were sold out, and Ra Ra Zoo received an offer to play at the New York Westside Arts Theatre in September. Until then, go and see them if you have the chance.

Plexus consists of Robert Morse, Jyl Hewston, and Joe Mori, who explain: "A 'Plexus' is created by the intertwining of different elements. Our work consists of combining the skills of three artists with very different experiences: in the fields of circus, dance, mime, music, martial arts, philosophy, American football, and car repair. None of us actually intended to enter the theater profession, and perhaps we still don't. But until then..." After a funny and well-performed first half based on mime and movement, the second half presented Plexus as "The Kurlytov Family Circus," a Russian troupe currently touring England. They spoke a kind of pseudo-Russian (I assume!), with occasional understandable words like circus, akrobatski. Having trouble opening the suitcase containing all their juggling props, they were forced to improvise: with cowbells and tambourines, scarves from an onion, a frying pan and a rubber chicken, with 3 basketballs and a hat. Interrupted by a funny incident with good, spontaneous remarks in response to a spectator who actually spoke Russian (KGBski), they finally managed to open the suitcase to retrieve 9 clubs, with which they performed in various 3-person formations. As a climax, Jyl passed to Robert and Joe while one stood on the other's shoulders.

In March, "Barnum" premiered in London's West End. The star of this "showbiz circus spectacular," loosely based on the life story of the legendary circus entrepreneur, is again Michael Crawford, who had made this role his own in the previous London season. Radiating charm and energy, he displays a wide range of well-mastered circus skills. The cast also includes Richard Gauntlet, who learned juggling and other circus techniques from Terry Williams at Clown Alley, Bromley, Kent. Terry offers workshops on Saturday afternoons. The trained acrobat also teaches juggling, unicycling, tightrope walking, and sells props.

The two most important juggling workshops in London at the Oval House and Jubilee Hall (see Juggling Meet) are growing, both in terms of participants and skills shown: 7-ball solo, 8-club passing, etc. The atmosphere is relaxed and friendly, welcoming both beginners and advanced jugglers. A visit is definitely worthwhile if you are in London.

An intensive course in circus techniques took place in March. Max and Sue (The Oddball Juggling Co) taught juggling. This course, where one could also learn acrobatics, balance, trapeze, and high wire, showed the potential for a permanent facility of this kind in London.

## In Our Own Words
**by Gabi**

Here we sit for the 3rd time amidst a mess of paper, letters, pencils, typewriter, Letraset, scissors, glue, coffee, and cake, and the result will be KASKADE No. 3, which you are holding. This No. 3 is a difficult birth, as we are not only sitting among stacks of Kaskade but also in a new, half-furnished apartment. Who doesn't know the chaos of moving! But for you, it just means: note our new address: Annastraße 7 6200 Wiesbaden Tel. 06121 425938

To celebrate the new apartment, we had a break-in last week while we were leading a juggling workshop at a theater meeting. Among other things, the thieves made off with the Kaskade cash box, which contained some cash and a lot of US $ checks that we always collect for a while to save costs and then cash together. A very annoying event and not exactly encouraging regarding Kaskade. (If I catch him, he'll get a club to the head!)

But there is also good news from us. Since the beginning of April, we have both left our jobs and are now full-time jugglers (completely infected and incurably ill, as Eddy Krzeptowski would say). This means we have a bit more time to take care of the ever-growing number of subscriptions. However, it does not mean that we will use our time to write Kaskade; above all, we don't want to do that. We want to be the collection point for all interesting news and pass it on to others. But we need your articles for that.

Each of you experiences stories related to juggling; many have experience with performances or workshops. Write them down, take photos, and send them to us. You can also send photos without stories, painted stories, whatever you want. We can only give suggestions on topics we would like to have. For example, we would like to report on circus schools or good circus art workshops. Also, write us your opinions: what do you think of Kaskade, what do you think of individual articles? For example, what do you think of the AEJ idea? Personally, I would like to hear more from jugglers. There are many, but so far, we have only received articles from jugglers. Furthermore, we are still missing event announcements. If you hear about theater, juggling, and other festivals, workshops, etc., please write to us.
